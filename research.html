TG-PLFNet: Interpretable RF Waveform Recognition

Manuscript under review

TG-PLFNet recognizes radar and communication waveforms directly from raw complex I/Q samples, without first turning them into spectrogram images. Its first layer is built from time-gated filters whose settings have a physical meaning, so we can read off where in time and frequency the network is looking.

The problem

Spectrum monitoring, cognitive radio, and electronic warfare systems must identify many kinds of RF emissions, from radar pulses to digital communication bursts, often at low signal-to-noise ratio. Classifiers that work on spectrogram images discard phase and fine timing, ordinary CNN filters say little about what the network has learned, and the useful information in short radar pulses sits in a small region of time and frequency.

Our approach

The network’s first layer is built from learnable filters whose few parameters have a direct physical meaning, so after training each filter can be read as a region of the time–frequency plane. A complex-valued network then classifies the filter outputs. We evaluate it on a large set of radar and communication waveforms and on over-the-air recordings from software-defined radios.

Beyond FFT: High-Resolution Radar Frequency Estimation

Manuscript under review

HarmoniNet estimates frequencies from short, noisy radar measurements where conventional FFT peaks merge. In stretch-processed radar each scatterer becomes a tone whose frequency gives its range, so sharper frequency estimates mean sharper range profiles, and better separation of targets that sit close together.

The problem

The FFT is fast, but with only a few samples its resolution is fixed by the record length. Returns closer than one frequency bin merge into a single peak, and a weak return can hide in the sidelobes of a strong one. Subspace and sparse methods can do better, but they need the number of components in advance and cost far more computation.

Our approach

HarmoniNet is a deep network that works directly on raw complex I/Q samples and reconstructs a dense, high-resolution spectrum, without being told how many returns are present. In our experiments it separates returns closer than the FFT’s resolution limit, holds up at low SNR, runs fast enough for real-time use, and gives clean range tracks on measured radar data.

Scorpion: Multimodal Sensing for Ground Robots

Scorpion is a U.S. Army ERDC project on multimodal ground robots for subterranean sensing. We use the Scorpion rover to study how complementary sensors can improve mapping and threat detection when visibility is limited or a single sensor gives an incomplete picture.

The problem

Caves, tunnels, and other underground spaces are often too dangerous to send people into, and they are hard to sense. Light ranges from bright to complete darkness, the air can be dusty or wet, line of sight breaks around corners, and walls create multipath reflections. Chemical and biological threats also vary in size, number, and signature, so no single sensor can reliably detect them all.

Our approach

We equip unmanned ground vehicles with complementary sensors: polarimetric and spectral cameras from ultraviolet to long-wave infrared, a 77 GHz millimeter-wave radar that records raw I/Q data for range, angle, and micro-Doppler, a multichannel acoustic array that locates sound sources, and LiDAR for mapping. Robots that mimic how biological threats move and sound let us run controlled lab experiments before field tests. We then compare what each sensor, and each combination, reveals, so we can recommend sensor suites for a given threat and power budget.

Current progress

Our platform is a Clearpath Husky rover carrying a 64-channel Ouster LiDAR, a TI AWR2243 millimeter-wave radar, a FLIR thermal camera, an Intel RealSense RGB-D camera, and a Sorama acoustic array. All sensors except the acoustic array run through ROS for synchronized, time-stamped recording and mapping, and the acoustic hotspots are aligned with the LiDAR map afterwards. The video below shows five synchronized views from one recording. In first fusion experiments, combining radar micro-Doppler spectrograms with thermal frames at the input classified eight human and vehicle classes with about 78% accuracy using a network of only about 111,000 parameters. Both results were presented at SPIE Autonomous Systems 2026.

Wi-Fi Sensing for Low-Cost Activity Monitoring at Home

Wi-Fi routers are already in almost every home. We study whether the channel state information (CSI) they produce can monitor daily activities, such as falls in older adults, without cameras or dedicated radar hardware.

The problem

Monitoring activity at home, for example to detect falls among older adults, should be private, inexpensive, and easy to install. Cameras raise privacy concerns and radar adds cost, while radar and Wi-Fi had not been compared under the same conditions.

Our approach

A Raspberry Pi 3B+ running Nexmon firmware captures Wi-Fi CSI while a phone generates traffic with the access point. A 77 GHz FMCW radar and a Kinect camera record the same scenes, so both sensors see identical activities. We turn both signals into micro-Doppler spectrograms, classify seven activities with CNNs, and release the datasets and code.

What we found and next steps

In matched experiments radar recognized the activities far more reliably, but Wi-Fi still detected them using hardware that costs a few dollars and is already in most homes. We also studied how robust Wi-Fi recognition is to changes in the setup. Ajaya Dahal, whose M.S. research I mentored, has since implemented the pipeline in real time on an AMD Versal FPGA. Next, we aim to close the accuracy gap with interpretable learnable filters on raw CSI, toward affordable in-home monitoring for healthy aging.

Future Directions

I want to understand how well radar models transfer across sensors, datasets, and environments. My next step is to study how differences in recording setups and radar representations affect that transfer, with the longer-term goal of building reusable models that adapt with fewer labeled examples.
